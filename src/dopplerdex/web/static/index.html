      How a sound can reveal where it was recorded

      Aircraft engines and propellers produce steady tones. As a plane flies past, the pitch you hear slides down. The exact shape of that slide depends on where you stand relative to the plane's path, and every plane's path is published as ADS-B flight data. Match the shape, and you match the place.

      1. The Doppler effect

      Sound travels at about 340 m/s. When a source approaches, each new wave starts a little closer to you, so the waves arrive bunched up and the pitch is higher. When it moves away, they arrive stretched out and the pitch is lower. The received frequency is

      f = f₀ · c / (c − vtoward you)

      where only the part of the aircraft's velocity pointing toward you counts. Directly overhead it moves sideways, so you hear exactly f₀.

      Speedsets how far the pitch drops: a faster plane drops further.
      Distancesets how quickly it drops: a close pass snaps down, a distant one glides.
      Timingsound needs time to reach you, so you hear each moment of the flight a few seconds late. Ignoring this produces a different curve, not just a shifted one.

      2. Try it

      Drag the flight path, change the speed, altitude, wind and microphone height. The lab synthesises the sound with the same physics the locator uses, draws its spectrogram and plays it.

      3. From a recording to a location

      1. Find the curves. The spectrogram is cleaned of steady hums and broadband noise; sliding tones are tracked and grouped into harmonics. Each pass is fitted for speed, closest distance and the moment it was heard, with honest error bars. Loud broadband swells (jets) and road traffic are recognised separately.
      2. Search every flight. For each aircraft in the area and time window, the places that would have heard its closest approach form two thin strips beside its track. Two events must agree on the same place and the same recording start time; a fast join finds those agreements across millions of possibilities. With only one aircraft curve, the strips of every matching flight become candidates and the time window plus the rest of the recording decide between them, which is less certain.
      3. Model the exact curve. For the surviving places, the true flight path (turns, climbs, speed changes) predicts the exact pitch curve you would hear there, including travel time, wind and temperature. It is compared with the detected curves.
      4. Check everything else. At the right place, every aircraft must fit the recording: predicted loud passes must be loud, predicted propeller curves must be present, and nothing should be predicted that is clearly absent. This decides between look-alike candidates.
      5. Refine. The winner is optimised continuously with detailed terrain and weather. On the reference challenge this lands within tens of metres.

      4. What makes a recording locatable

      Length30-60 minutes. More passes mean more constraints.
      StillnessPhone lying still, not in a hand or pocket. Handling noise hides faint curves.
      WindShelter the microphone; wind rumble masks the low tones that matter most.
      AircraftPropeller planes within ~5 km, or jets within ~3 km at low altitude. Near an airport is ideal.
      TimeNote the start time. A tight time window makes even a single pass useful.

      5. Limits and privacy

      The method needs aircraft that broadcast ADS-B. In Europe many light aircraft are not required to, and coverage at low altitude depends on volunteer receivers nearby. Short recordings, heavy wind or handling, and places far from any flight path often cannot be located. The confidence margin tells you how clearly the best answer beat the alternatives.

      Everything runs on this computer. Use it only on recordings you made yourself or have permission to analyse: a precise location can reveal where someone lives.
